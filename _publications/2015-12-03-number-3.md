Background: Bereavement, i.e., the death of significant others, generally arouses grief reactions and may be accompanied by feelings of guilt and shame. However, it remains unclear whether guilt and shame can influence grief adaptation through the process of meaning making (MM), and the extent of their effects on grief symptoms may differ, especially among naturally bereaved population. Therefore, this study aims to examine the differential roles of guilt and shame in grief adaptation through MM among bereaved Chinese adults.


Methods: Participants were recruited online through a convenience sampling method. Data was collected through online questionnaire at three time points. In total, 396 bereaved adults completed all the surveys, self-reporting their grief, guilt, shame, and MM. Descriptive statistics were conducted in SPSS. Subsequently, cross-lagged panel analysis was performed in Mplus to construct models for guilt/shame, MM, and grief symptoms, and the bootstrap method was used to test the mediating effect of MM.


Results: The path coefficients indicated that guilt did not predict subsequent levels of grief symptoms or MM. In the model for shame, T1 shame negatively predicted T2 MM, which in turn negatively predicted T3 shame but not T3 grief. As a result, the mediating effect of MM was found to be nonsignificant in both models.


Conclusions: The results indicate that shame has a stronger negative effect on MM and grief adjustment than guilt. To assist bereaved individuals in coping with grief, clinicians should help those experiencing high levels of shame to more accurately evaluate their responsibilities.
